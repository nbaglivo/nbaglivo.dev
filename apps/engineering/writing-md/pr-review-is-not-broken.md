---
title: "Pr review is not broken"
date: "Oct 29, 2026"
---

Yes, since a few months ago every post on Linkedin has been about how PR review is broken. The angle these posts take varies from things like the practicality of reviewing a 10k lines diff and the time it takes, the challenges of not falling on cognitive debt by having just shallow reviews and even social aspects of software development like [code review surrender and exploitation](https://www.michaelagreiler.com/codereview-surrender-exploitation/)

Yes, I'm going to add one more thing to the endless bucket of posts. Like many other people, I always thought that code review was a horrible way of catching bugs, and an awful, still somewhat effective way, of catching drift on architecture and misalignment. Still too late for my taste, this type of misalignment should have been caught earlier, before any code was written, so code review was kinda the last chance before this ships and we move on, possibly not seeing this for a long time. So what was code review good for then? I think it was somewhat good for less experienced engineers to see how certain technical problems were solved (so not really review but more just "reading"?) and then also good for narcissistic engineers to nitpick and impose their preference on the code base. Not much more beyond that, really.

Many things have changed this year, one of them being that coding is relatively cheap to build (at least in time-cost terms), so if we catch a drift during review, it is not so terrible as it was before when an engineer had been working on this during two weeks. PRs are also bigger now, so good luck catching drift on a 10k PR, reverse engineering of all of that into the actual mental model (mental model that the AI that created it does not have). But as I said, PR review was terrible for this anyways and good teams did not rely on it for this kind of thing, AI made it worse, sure, so a bigger problem for teams that relied solely on PR review to ensure architecture compliance. 

The other thing is the reading, learning, aspect of it for less experienced engineers, I think that one is interesting but I won't get into that one just now, I think it will probably become its own post.

What I'm here to say is that we don't have a PR review problem, we have a trust problem. Before, we were asked to trust people, now we are asked to trust models, and that is proving to be difficult. The reason it's been difficult is that AI makes decisions we don't like and that they even "lie" about them, they can promise to follow certain patterns and then do the complete opposite. And the worst part is that they can never earn our trust, because they might do good work today, but a terrible one tomorrow, no consistency, no such thing as building confidence. So engineers have been trying to build guardrails, focusing on verification, doing what they can around the harness. Labs have also improved their models to absorb all these learnings in their training and their newer models are generally considered (no data here, just my perception) more trustworthy. Still, it is hard to trust these things and that's why so many engineers are skeptical about dark factories on serious and critical systems.

I'm going to end this post here, just acknowledging that I'm not sure how to solve this problem, I just have a bunch of questions. Are we ever going to be able to trust these things so we can let go of review? Is the answer control and verification instead of trust? Why do we even trust humans? I mean, everybody makes mistakes... is it fundamentally an empathy thing? can we feel empathetic towards models? Do we even care if they make mistakes? do we even care if there is a mental model they follow? I mean, as these things become cheaper and better to regenerate code, can we just throw away things and start again every time? Lots of questions, I could go on forever, I invite you to come up with your questions as well.
